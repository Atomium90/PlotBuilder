# PlotBuilder: Technical Documentation

Technical documentation for PlotBuilder, companion to the GDD (the design source of truth): the construction and structural-portance systems (grid-based building, inventory, shop), the demolition systems built on top of them (durability and materials, the four demolition tools, damage-coupled stability, the staggered collapse queue and fracture-on-destroy), and the game loop around them (the house generator, the mission level and its contract binding, the offers and the client's report, the hub, the saved progress, the player's home and the paint brush). Covers the overall architecture, the main classes and components, how the interaction system works, the physics and construction systems, demolition, the structural portance graph, the inventory and shop, generation, missions, the hub and the home, the UI layer, the Chaos setup, the debugging tools, and the main technical choices made.

## Architecture

`APlayerCharacter` carries independent components, each with a single responsibility, plus a `UBuildModeComponent` layered on top of two of them. `APlot` bounds the buildable area and owns the structural portance graph.

| Class | Role |
|---|---|
| `APlayerCharacter` | Movement, camera, Enhanced Input bindings, and the per-map switches (`SetBuildingAllowed`, `SetDestructionAllowed`) that say what the player may do in a level |
| `UInteractionComponent` | Traces from the view, tracks the focused actor, relays to `IInteractable` |
| `IInteractable` | Generic contract (`OnFocusBegin`/`OnFocusEnd`/`Interact`) - implementable in C++ or Blueprint |
| `UPhysicsManipulationComponent` | The Hands tool: grab and throw on the current focus, acting directly on its physics body (plus a leftover push, to be removed) |
| `UHotbarComponent` | Stacking grid inventory (36 slots: a 9-slot hotbar row plus a backpack), the item "on the cursor", money, shop purchase, acquisition (Interact/Purchase), instant placement for `APhysicsProp`, and the shared selection state for both Prop and Buildable flows |
| `UShopCartComponent` | The shop cart: the rows and quantities the player means to buy, kept until confirmed; the prices, the money and the room in the bag stay `UHotbarComponent`'s |
| `UBuildModeComponent` | Placement flow for `ABuildablePiece`: ghost preview, rotation, obstruction and stability checks, through one of two placement paths (flush-snap or grid) |
| `ABuildablePiece` | Construction piece: mesh, build anchor, declared sockets, load-bearing flag; never simulates physics. Its price is its row in `DT_Shop`, not a field |
| `APhysicsProp` | Simulated physics object (crate, furniture...), carried with the hands (grab and throw) or set down in build mode |

No object-specific logic lives in `APlayerCharacter` - it only knows about its own components, which in turn only know about `IInteractable` and raw physics bodies, never about a specific object class.

## Interaction system

`UInteractionComponent` traces every tick from the controller's view point on a dedicated collision channel (`GameTraceChannel2`, named "Interactable" in the collision profile settings). If the trace hits an actor implementing `IInteractable`, it becomes the current focus (`OnFocusBegin`/`OnFocusEnd` fire on change) and `TryInteract()` calls `Interact()` on it. Both `ABuildablePiece` and `APhysicsProp` implement `Interact` to store themselves into the instigator's `UHotbarComponent` - the component itself has no idea what kind of object it just picked up. A piece placed in the grid goes back through `UHotbarComponent::TakeBack`: the bag is checked first (a full bag leaves the piece standing, with a message), the unit goes in at the scale the piece was placed with rather than its biased actor scale, and a mirror variant goes back as the piece Build Mode swaps it in for, so it stacks with the bought ones. Only then does the piece leave the structural graph and destroy itself. A piece that holds others up (the same dry run as the collapse warning, `WouldBeBottleneck`) does not leave on one press: the first press says why nothing happened and arms `FTakeBackConfirm`, and a second press on the same piece within `TakeBackConfirmSeconds` (2 s) takes it. The guard is a pure struct that is given the time, so it is tested without a level. In a mission the hotbar is off and `Interact` on a placed piece does nothing. In build mode the interact key is ignored (`APlayerCharacter::InteractInput`), so a piece cannot be taken back while the player is building. Door leaves also implement the interface, to open and close.

## Inventory and shop

### Inventory

`UHotbarComponent` holds the inventory as a flat array of `FInventorySlot` (`ActorClass`, `Scale`, `Count`, `StackSize`). The grid is `NumColumns` x `NumRows` (9 x 4): the slot at `Row * NumColumns + Column`, row 0 being the hotbar and the rows below the backpack. A separate cursor slot holds the stack currently "in hand".

Interaction follows the Minecraft model, implemented once in `OnSlotLeftClicked(SlotIndex)` and `OnSlotRightClicked(SlotIndex)`: a left click picks up, places, merges identical stacks or swaps different ones; a right click places a single item. The selection (`GetSelectedIndex`, `CycleSelection`) is bound to the 9 hotbar slots.

The component exposes read access (`GetSlots`, `GetCursorSlot`, `GetSelectedIndex`, `GetMoney`, `IsInfiniteMoney`, `CountOf`, `GetFreeSlotCount`, `GetTotalSlotCount`) and five events without parameters (`OnInventoryChanged`, `OnSelectedSlotChanged`, `OnCursorSlotChanged`, `OnMoneyChanged`, and `OnAllowedChanged`, fired when a map turns the building side on or off). The UI does not poll for state: it re-reads it through the getters whenever one of these events fires.

### Shop catalog

`UHotbarComponent::ShopCatalog` is a `DataTable` of `FShopItemRow` - one row per purchasable item, Buildable or Prop alike: `DisplayName`, `ActorClass`, `Cost`, a `Category` (the `EShopItemCategory` enum: Structure, Opening, Roof, Prop and Paint, used for the shop tabs and the colour of an item's icon), and a stack size. `PurchaseRow` checks the player's money, debits it and adds the item to the inventory with no world actor involved, as opposed to `Interact` picking up something already physically placed in the world. The component keeps a per-map copy of the money and writes every change back to `UProgressSubsystem`, which is the source of truth across maps, and it takes a change made elsewhere back from it (`HandleProgressChanged`: a payment is credited to the progress directly, and without this the copy, and the HUD that reads it, would keep the amount the map started with); it has an infinite-money mode for testing. The catalog is also the one source of prices: the mission binding weighs a piece or a prop by its row's `Cost`, and a piece has no price field of its own (the old `Cost` field of `ABuildablePiece` was never read and has been removed). Adding a purchasable item is a new DataTable row, not new code.

A row can cap how many of its item the player holds (`MaxOwned`, 0 for no limit). Every paint bucket row sets it to 1, because a bucket stacks to 1 and a stack of identical colours would only fill the bag. `UHotbarComponent::GetPurchaseLimit` returns what is still allowed, counting what the grid and the cursor already hold.

### Cart

The shop sells through a cart, not item by item. `UShopCartComponent` lives on the player and holds the lines (a row name and a quantity). It broadcasts `OnCartChanged` and `OnPurchased`. `StepQuantity` adds or removes one unit, or ten while Shift is held (read from `FSlateApplication`), and caps a line at the row's purchase limit. Whether the cart can be paid is not its business. `UHotbarComponent::QuoteLines` answers it as an `FCartQuote` (units, total cost, slots needed, free slots, affordable, fits), by placing the items on a copy of the grid with the same rules as a real purchase. `Confirm` then calls `PurchaseLines`, which buys every line or none: a cart that costs too much, or does not fit in the bag, changes nothing. The cart survives the shop being closed and is empty on every map. `PurchaseRow` stays for a single item (the `Buy <index>` console command).

## Physics: the hands (grab and throw)

`UPhysicsManipulationComponent` is what the Hands tool (belt slot 0) does: primary use grabs, secondary use throws, which is how the player carries furniture out of a house to the client's drop zone. It reads the sibling `UInteractionComponent`'s current focus (no separate trace) and acts on its physics body directly:
- **Grab** picks up the focus if it has a simulated body and nothing is already held. A single press, nothing to keep pressed. A prop hung on a wall is taken off it first (`APhysicsProp::Release`).
- **Throw** lets go of what is held with an impulse along the view (`LaunchImpulseStrength`), and does nothing when the hands are empty. It is the only way to put the object down.
- While held, a spring pulls the object towards a point `HoldDistance` (200 cm) in front of the view, damped against its current velocity to stop oscillation. `GetHeldActor` tells the notebook whether a piece of furniture is carried or still in the house.
- **Push** (`Push`, `PushFocus`) is a leftover of the first interaction tool, which had Push, Pull and Launch on one contextual button. Nothing in the game shoves an object any more, so it is **to be removed**. Until then only the hub's older binding calls it, because the belt is locked there (see "What a map allows").

The spring targets the body's **center of mass**, not its component origin - targeting the origin caused off-center meshes to spin continuously, since the pivot-to-center-of-mass offset rotates with the object and fed rotational energy back into the spring.

## Demolition

### Durability and materials

Anything a tool can damage implements `IDamageable`, a single-function contract (`ApplyDamage(FDamageInfo)`) - a tool resolves a hit, builds an `FDamageInfo` (amount, tool id, hit location, impulse) and calls it on whatever it traced, without ever knowing if that's a wall, a post or (later) a piece of furniture. `ABuildablePiece::ApplyDamage_Implementation` just forwards to its own `UDurabilityComponent`.

`UDurabilityComponent` owns the HP, the material (`EDemolitionMaterial`: Wood, Plaster, Brick, Concrete, Glass, Metal) and the damage state (`EDurabilityState`: Intact, Damaged, Critical, Destroyed), and knows nothing about its owner. `InitializeFromRules` computes `MaxHP` once from `DA_DemolitionRules` (`MaterialBaseHP[Material] x PieceClassFactor[PieceClass]`, the piece's role - foundation, exterior wall, stairs... - factoring HP independently of its material); `ApplyDamage` subtracts, recomputes the state against the rules' `DamagedThreshold`/`CriticalThreshold`, and broadcasts `OnDurabilityDamaged`/`OnDurabilityStateChanged`/`OnDurabilityDestroyed`. `ABuildablePiece` listens to all three purely to react (damage tint, fracture-on-destroy) - the component itself never touches the mesh or the structural graph.

The damage tint is a `Damage` scalar (`1 - GetHPRatio()`) written to the mesh's Custom Primitive Data (index 1), read by the piece's material - the same per-instance-data technique `UStructuralSupportComponent`'s stability heat-map uses (index 0), so neither needs its own material permutation per piece.

The tool x material matrix (`ToolMaterialMatrix` on `DA_DemolitionRules`, one `FToolMaterialMultipliers` row per `EDemolitionTool`) answers "how much does this tool's base damage actually do to this material" - `ComputeDamage(Tool, Material, BaseDamage)` is the one place that logic lives, called by every tool so a new tool or a re-tuned matchup is a data change, not new code.

### Toolbelt and tools

`UToolbeltComponent` is deliberately separate from `UHotbarComponent`: construction pieces and props are stock the player owns, tools are equipped gear. Slot 0 is Hands (routes straight to the sibling `UPhysicsManipulationComponent`: primary Grabs, secondary Throws - a narrower pair than the component's leftover Push (to be removed), which stays reachable while the belt is locked, through `APlayerCharacter`'s locked-toolbelt gate). Slot 1 (Charge) spawns and throws an `AExplosiveCharge` on secondary use, or sticks it on the surface in reach on primary use, then detonates it on the next secondary press. Slots 2-4 are `ADemolitionTool` instances (Sledgehammer/Crowbar/Jackhammer), spawned once in `BeginPlay` and kept for the owner's lifetime, unlike the consumable charge. Slot 5 is the paint brush: primary use forwards to the sibling `UPaintBrushComponent`, the way slot 0 forwards to the physics component. The brush comes last so that adding it renumbered nothing. For the interface the belt answers what a tool tile needs: `OnBeltChanged` (fired when the equipped tool or what a map allows changes), the name and the hint of a slot (`GetSlotName`, `GetSlotHint`, what the two clicks do), whether the player owns it (`IsSlotOwned`, see "Buying the tools" below), how many are missing (`CountMissingTools`) and what a slot costs to keep (`GetUpkeepForTool`, read from the data asset of the tool class).

**What a map allows.** Three switches, independent of each other, decide which slots can be held, so a level mixes them freely:

| Switch | Set by | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Belt lock (`UToolbeltComponent::SetLocked`) | the hub | Only the Hands remain, and the legacy Grab/Push bindings (to be removed) take over |
| Building (`APlayerCharacter::SetBuildingAllowed`) | the hub and the mission | Build mode, the hotbar and the shop go off, and so does the paint brush: painting is part of building |
| Destruction (`APlayerCharacter::SetDestructionAllowed`) | the home | The charge and the three melee tools cannot be equipped |

**Buying the tools.** A fourth setting, `UToolbeltComponent::SetOwnershipEnforced` (through `APlayerCharacter::SetToolOwnershipEnforced`), is not a switch of what the map allows but of whether the tools are something to buy. When it is on, `IsSlotOwned` asks `UProgressSubsystem::OwnsTool` for the three melee slots, and a tool the player has not bought is drawn as a padlock on the belt and cannot be equipped. The hands, the paint brush and the charge slot are never sold: the charge is a stock (`UProgressSave::Charges`, capped by the `MaxStock` of its row in `DT_ToolShop`; the stepper and the purchase stop at the room left), and every throw or placement takes one (`TakeCharge`; a placement that misses the surface costs nothing, a spawn that fails gives it back, and with none left a message says so). The slot stays on the belt whatever the stock, so a charge already out can still be detonated. The hub, mission and home directors turn it on (`bEnforceToolOwnership`, default on); a map with no director, such as `Lvl_DemoTest`, keeps every tool and unlimited charges. The belt listens to `UProgressSubsystem::OnProgressChanged`, so a purchase at the PC shows at once, and an equipped tool that is no longer usable falls back to the hands.

`UToolbeltComponent::IsSlotAvailable` is the one place that combines them: the Hands always, every other slot unless the belt is locked or its own switch is off. The wheel skips what is unavailable, and an item in hand goes back to the Hands when its switch turns off. Today the hub allows nothing, a mission allows demolition only, the home allows building and painting only, and the test map allows everything. A renovation contract, which may ask the player to rebuild and repaint as well as demolish, is the same code with building and destruction both on, set by that map's director.

`ADemolitionTool` is one C++ class for all three melee tools - `BP_SledgeHammer`/`BP_Crowbar`/`BP_JackHammer` differ only by their `UDemolitionToolData` (base damage, rate, range, hit radius, wind-up, `bContinuous`, camera shake class) and their held mesh/socket offset. `PrimaryUse` traces from the owner's view, resolves `IDamageable` targets (the traced actor alone, or everything in a radius around the impact point if `HitRadius > 0`, mirroring the charge's own overlap loop), and gates re-use behind one busy window covering wind-up plus the rest of the `1/Rate` cycle. A continuous tool (the jackhammer) instead starts a looping timer at `1/Rate` on press and stops it on `ReleasePrimaryUse` (LMB up) - no separate base class needed, `bContinuous` branches inside the same `PrimaryUse`.

A tool can cost money to keep. `FToolUpkeep` is data on `UDemolitionToolData`: a flat fee for each mission in which the tool worked, plus a rate for each second of active use (two parts, because a flat fee alone ignores how hard the tool was used and a rate alone turns a long drilling session into most of the payout). Every hit that lands counts for `1 / Rate` seconds, which `ADemolitionTool` reports to `UMissionResultSubsystem::RecordToolActivity` when the tool has a fee, so a continuous tool counts the time it is actually hitting something. The report charges it (see "Evaluator and result"). A tool whose fee is zero reports nothing.

`AExplosiveCharge` is a physics actor (same mesh/collision setup as `APhysicsProp`): thrown with an initial velocity, settles under gravity, and detonates on a second press (`RequestDetonation`, after `DetonationDelay` for the beep/warning). `Detonate` applies flat damage (no falloff) to every `IDamageable` in `Radius` through the same `ComputeDamage` matrix lookup as the melee tools, plus a flat radial impulse to nearby simulating bodies. A translucent sphere scaled to `Radius` previews the blast area for the charge's whole armed lifetime, doubling as the gameplay indicator and the visual debug tool.

Hit feedback (camera shake + a per-material impact sound, both data-driven) and fracture are shared, not per-tool: every tool ends in the same `IDamageable::ApplyDamage` call, so `UDurabilityComponent` is the single place a hit becomes a state change regardless of which of the four tools caused it.

A prop is also hurt by a hard contact (GDD §10.2), with no tool involved. `APhysicsProp` listens to its mesh's `OnComponentHit` and divides the contact impulse by the body's mass, which is the speed change the contact gave it, whatever hit it: a wall stopping a thrown chair, a slab landing on a sofa. Above `ImpactSpeedThreshold` (600 cm/s, so a 1 m drop of about 440 cm/s is harmless) it deals `(speed - threshold) * ImpactDamagePerSpeed` through the same `ApplyDamage` call, with the tool id `Impact`. Two guards keep it honest: contacts are ignored for `ImpactGraceSeconds` after `BeginPlay` (a prop dropped by the generator, the home restore or the hotbar settles against the floor), and a damaging contact starts an `ImpactMinInterval` cooldown (one bounce reports several contacts). The four values and `bTakesImpactDamage` are class defaults, tuned per Blueprint; with `pb.Debug 1` each damaging contact prints its speed and damage.

## Construction: Build Mode

Placing a `APhysicsProp` is instant (`UHotbarComponent::TryPlace`, grid-snapped). Placing a `ABuildablePiece` goes through a separate, toggleable **Build Mode** (`UBuildModeComponent`), since blind instant placement was tested and found unusable. A single reusable ghost actor (mesh/material swapped in place, never respawned) previews the selected piece. It is tinted green when placement is comfortably stable, yellow when it would fall under the comfort threshold (fragile - it may or may not actually collapse, see "Unstable placement" below), and red when blocked.

Each `ABuildablePiece` declares a build anchor (`EBuildAnchor`: `Legacy`, `Cell`, `Edge`, `Vertex`) that selects one of two placement paths. `Legacy` is the default, so any piece not migrated keeps the flush-snap behaviour unchanged.

### Legacy path: flush snap

- One trace per tick decides the ghost's transform: hits a placed `ABuildablePiece` → snaps flush against it (face picked from where the hit lands within the target's own local bounding box, not just the raw impact normal, so aiming near an edge is enough); hits anything else solid → rests flush there instead (ground, level geometry, a Prop); hits nothing → falls back to a fixed distance in front of the view.
- Rotation (45° steps) is manual (two keys) and stacks on top of whatever base rotation the snap implies.
- One box overlap per tick (world statics, dynamics, pawns) at the candidate transform decides the blocked state - it ignores the ghost, the piece being snapped onto, and the player, and shrinks the test box by a small tolerance so two flush, touching pieces don't false-positive from floating-point error.

### Grid path

The flush snap places a piece against another piece's bounding box, which cannot put a wall on a foundation's edge or a post on a vertex. The grid path places a piece on an integer grid instead, so that its sockets land exactly on its neighbours' sockets.

- **Units.** A floor cell (`CellSize`) is 200 cm, which is 2 grid units `u` of 100 cm. Cell centres are at (even, even), edge midpoints at (odd, even) or (even, odd), vertices at (odd, odd). `FloorHeight` is 300 cm.
- **Anchors.** `Cell`: foundation, floor slab, roof tiles. `Edge`: walls, interior walls, openings, gables. `Vertex`: corner posts.
- **Where the ghost goes.** The view ray is intersected with a horizontal plane at the build level's height (the level is changed with two input actions), and the hit point is snapped to the nearest cell centre, edge midpoint or vertex of the anchor type. The pivot is what gets placed, never the centre of the bounds; the sockets are expressed relative to the pivot.
- **Heights.** The top of the level-0 foundation is at `GroundZ + 20`; a piece at level `k` has its pivot at that height plus `k x 300`. Exterior walls are 300 cm, so a wall runs up through the 20 cm of the slab above it and its top is at the logical level of the next storey: roof pieces need no vertical offset (`VerticalOffset` is 0 everywhere, the field remains for pieces that need one). `GroundZ` is the plot actor's own Z.
- **Wall side.** Exterior walls have their pivot on one face, so a wall sits entirely on one side of its edge instead of straddling it: it never overhangs the foundation, and two walls meeting at a corner overlap inside the footprint without needing a post. A `bFaceAnchoredEdge` flag selects this behaviour per piece; the side is the one that has floor under it, or the aimed side if both do. Interior walls stay centred on their edge.
- **Integer bookkeeping.** A placed piece stores its grid coordinate (cell and level) and its orientation in quarter turns. Everything downstream - occupancy, socket matching, stability - compares integers, never floats.
- **Occupancy.** `UStructuralSupportComponent` keeps a table of occupied slots, keyed by slot type (floor, edge, vertex), cell and level. One slot holds one piece. This replaces the physical overlap test between pieces, which would otherwise reject two walls overlapping at a corner. A roof tile in a cell slot lives in its own layer of that table (and is keyed by its `RoofStep`), so a ceiling slab and the first row of roof tiles that rests on the same walls do not share a slot.
- **Blocked or not.** The ghost is red when the position is outside the plot, the slot is occupied, or something other than a piece (the player, a prop, the environment) is in the way. The overlap box is cut at `GroundZ` so the buried part of a foundation never counts as an obstruction.
- **Risky placement.** A candidate under the comfort threshold is allowed, not blocked (yellow) - two different thresholds are at play: the ghost turns yellow as soon as stability drops under `ComfortThreshold` (0.5), but the piece only actually collapses if it's also under the stricter `CollapseThreshold` (0.1). A single horizontal link from an anchor, for instance, lands at exactly 0.4 - yellow, but stable, since 0.4 is still above 0.1. A candidate that does end up under 0.1 is placed, then collapses right after, exactly like a removal-triggered collapse.
- **Overlap fixes (visual only, nothing in the portance logic).** Pieces that touch share faces and overlap by the thickness of a wall, so small corrections are applied in `APlot::PlacePiece` and after registration. An exterior wall is 300 cm, so it runs up through the slab above it and hides the slab edge. A wall or a post is `TopFaceBias` lower than the slab above, so no top face is coplanar with it. At a corner, an exterior wall is a hair longer or shorter by the parity of its orientation (`CornerParityBias`) and a partition is always shorter. A floor slab or a stair next to an exterior wall is pulled back by the wall's thickness (`SlabWallRetreat`, `StairWallRetreat`, only 1 cm on a side that is walled for part of its length): `UStructuralSupportComponent::RefreshWallClearance` reads the edge occupancy table when a wall is placed or removed and `ABuildablePiece::ApplyWallClearance` scales and moves the piece, so a demolished wall gives the piece its full size back. A depth offset in the material stays a last resort.

### Prop path

A third path is for props (`APhysicsProp`, the furniture of the hotbar). A click on a prop outside build mode points to build mode instead of dropping it, and in build mode the wheel visits props as well as pieces. A prop is not placed on the grid but where the view lands:
- **Where.** The view is traced on the interaction channel. A floor-like hit (normal pointing up) is where the mesh's bottom rests, rounded to `PropSnapSize` (10 cm). A steeper hit (a wall) puts the box of the mesh flush against it, on the floor at the wall's foot, within `PropDropDistance`. The prop is placed from the box of its mesh, never from its pivot, which sits at a corner for most of the kit.
- **Yaw.** Turned by `PropRotationStepDegrees` (15°), finer than a piece, since furniture is arranged by eye.
- **Hanging.** A prop that hangs (the mirror and the cabinet) goes on a plain wall piece at the aimed height, back to the wall. How a class is set down (hung or standing, which way its front faces) is read from `DT_FurnishingRules` by `APhysicsProp::GetPlacement`, so the generator, the build mode and the home restore share one source.
- **Blocked or not.** The obstruction test is the legacy one, ignoring the surface the prop stands on.
- **Confirming.** Spawns the prop at the ghost and calls `APhysicsProp::Place(Support)`: the body stops simulating, so the prop stays where it was put and the hands cannot grab it, and when the support is a piece of the kit the prop is mounted on it (see "Wall props"), so damage, collapse or removal of the piece lets it fall. The ground, the level or another prop hold it for good. A prop is not registered in the structural graph. The home restore sets each prop down the same way, finding its support from the prop's own box, so furniture no longer drops and tumbles at every visit.

## Structural portance

### The piece kit

Pieces are greybox meshes on a 200 cm cell, one mesh per piece; a Blueprint per piece assigns the mesh and sets the data (anchor, sockets, load-bearing flag, cost). Walls are 300 cm high and run up through the 20 cm floor slab above them, so one storey is 300 cm.

| Group | Pieces |
|---|---|
| Structure | foundation (also the ground-floor floor), floor slab, plain wall, wall with door opening, wall with window opening (single, large and wide), corner post, stairs |
| Child Actors | door leaf, window pane: separate Blueprints carried by the wall Blueprints, not placeable pieces |
| Interior | interior wall (10 cm), interior wall with door opening |
| Roof (one slope, 1:2 = 26.6°) | slope, ridge, flat top tile, hip corner, valley corner, half gable (and its mirror), gable infill, ridge cap (`Tent`), pyramid, hip end, gable peak |

A door leaf is a Child Actor of the door-wall Blueprints, driven by a Timeline. A window pane works the same way: `BP_WindowGlass` is a Child Actor of the three window-wall Blueprints (`BP_WallWindow`, `BP_WallWindowLarge`, `BP_WallWindowWide`), one 100 x 100 cm mesh stretched to the opening of each wall. Neither carries a structural socket or registers in the graph (`GetRegisteredPieces` skips child actors), so opening a door or breaking a pane changes nothing in the portance. They differ in what they do with the player. The leaf replaces `Interact` to open and close. A pane is an `ABuildablePiece` of class `WindowPane` (always glass, HP factor 1.0) with a `UDurabilityComponent` of its own, so the glass is damaged and breaks separately from the wall, into its own Geometry Collection (see "Chaos setup"). A pane is not an item of its own: `ABuildablePiece::Interact` forwards to the parent actor, so aiming at the glass and pressing interact acts on the wall that carries it. Stairs are supported from below and, like other structural pieces, are load-bearing: a floor landing at the top of a flight can rest on them. Their collision is the mesh itself.

### Sockets

A piece does not store its connections on its mesh. Its Blueprint declares an array of `FStructuralSocket` (a type, a `CellOffset` relative to the piece's pivot in grid units, and a `RoofStep`), so swapping a mesh never touches the portance logic. The graph does not read the mesh at all.

| Socket | Carried by | Connects to |
|---|---|---|
| `Anchor` | foundation | the terrain; stability 1.0 |
| `FloorEdge` | foundation, floor slab (4 edge midpoints) | `WallBottom`; a neighbouring `FloorEdge` |
| `FloorCorner` | foundation, floor slab (4 corners) | the `WallBottom` of a post only |
| `WallBottom` / `WallTop` / `WallSide` | walls, post | the floor or the wall below / the floor, the roof or the wall above / the neighbouring wall |
| `FloorBottom` | floor slab (4 edges and 4 corners) | `WallTop` |
| `RoofLow` / `RoofHigh` / `RoofSide` | roof tiles | the wall below or the previous tile / the next tile / a lateral neighbour |

`FloorCorner` is a separate type so that two slabs touching only at a corner are not considered joined. Door slots, the door leaf and the window panes carry no structural socket.

Socket positions are turned into world-independent integer coordinates by one shared function (the piece's grid coordinate plus its offset rotated by its orientation), used by Build Mode, the debug view and the graph, so that a mismatch is visible before it is misinterpreted by the portance logic.

### Roof step

Roof pieces rise in 100 cm steps, which is not a divisor of the 300 cm storey. Sockets therefore carry a second, independent vertical axis, `RoofStep`, and so do placed pieces; two sockets only match if their absolute `RoofStep` values are strictly equal. It serves two cases: a tile climbing a slope (`RoofLow` and `RoofHigh` at different steps on the same piece) and a gable infill stacking on another one. It is 0 on every non-roof socket, so it never changes what a floor level means for any other piece. The height of one step is a tunable (`RoofStepHeight`, 100 cm).

### Propagation

Each placed piece is a node and each pair of matching sockets a link. Stability is a value from 0 to 1 that starts at 1.0 on `Anchor` and propagates outwards: a piece takes the best value among its neighbours minus a loss that depends on the link's class (vertical, horizontal, or roof). Below a threshold, or when no chain of links leads back to an anchor, the piece collapses; its removal can in turn cut other pieces off, which is how a cascade happens. The losses and thresholds live in `DA_StructuralRules`, so they are tuned without recompiling. A floor slab does not only pass the value along: it restarts the chain for what stands on it (see "Floors restart the chain").

Load-bearing is a flag on the piece, not a mesh: a load-bearing wall transmits stability to what rests on it (floor, roof), while a partition receives its stability from the floor it stands on and transmits nothing upward. Walls with openings and roof pieces are load-bearing, interior walls are not. Roof pieces being load-bearing is what lets stability climb tile by tile up a slope (`RoofHigh` to the next tile's `RoofLow`) - a roof is stable only while an unbroken chain of links leads from a tile's `RoofLow` back to the `WallTop` of a load-bearing wall.

Pair classes that are not obvious: `WallTop` to `RoofLow` is vertical, and so is `FloorEdge` to `RoofLow`: the first row of a roof, and its gable pieces, rest on the edge of the ceiling slab as much as on the wall top, so the ceiling slab under a roof restarts the chain the roof starts from; the roof-to-roof pairs (`RoofLow`/`RoofLow`, `RoofHigh`/`RoofHigh`, `RoofHigh`/`RoofLow`, `RoofSide`/`RoofSide`) are horizontal, so a roof held only by other roof tiles, never reaching a load-bearing wall, is not stable.

The graph is recomputed on every placement and removal, but no longer inline: `RegisterPiece`/`UnregisterPiece` just mark the graph dirty (`MarkDirty`), and `TickComponent` does the actual relaxation at most once per frame. A cascade that unregisters N pieces in the same frame (collapse releases, see below) recomputes once, not N times. The same evaluation (with the candidate piece's sockets, in integer coordinates) answers `WouldBeStable`, which drives the ghost's yellow state.

### Floors restart the chain, and stacked walls

Two rules keep tall and open buildings possible (GDD §8, D28, D37).

**Floors restart the chain.** With a vertical loss of 0.1 and a collapse threshold of 0.1, a stack of walls and slabs in which every link only loses stability reaches the threshold around the fifth storey, so a tall building could not stand even at full health. A floor slab therefore does more than pass a value along. What stands on it (a wall's `WallBottom`, the low edge of a roof tile; `IsRestingOnSlab`) receives the slab's stability as 1.0 instead of its real value, the way a foundation's `Anchor` starts at 1.0, as long as the slab itself stands: its own stability has to reach `SubAnchorMinimumIncomingStability` (0.1, equal to the collapse threshold). A wall on a slab at full health therefore starts at 0.9 (1.0 times the slab's damage coupling `c(h)`, minus the vertical loss), whatever the storey.
- It is a flag on the piece, `bResetsStability`, set on the floor slab Blueprint (`BP_Floor`), not a piece of its own. Every floor slab of a generated house, the ceiling slab under a roof and a slab the player places behave the same, with the same footprint and the same 20 cm height as before, so a storey stays 300 cm. The flag keeps the names of the first design in the code (sub-anchor, `SubAnchorMinimumIncomingStability`): it started as a second slab placed every few storeys, and became the floor slab itself on 2026-11-11.
- The slab's own stability is worked out like any floor's, from what is under it and beside it, and it takes nothing back from what stands on it (otherwise the slab would prop itself up through the wall it carries). A slab whose support gives out falls as a floor does, and a slab with no chain below it holds nothing up.
- So the height of a building is not limited by the losses any more: each storey needs an unbroken chain down to the slab under it, and that slab an unbroken chain of its own.

**Stacked walls.** A `WallTop` also matches the `WallBottom` of the wall stacked on it on the same edge, as a vertical link with the same loss as the others. A wall bordering a stairwell hole has no slab beneath or above it, so without this link it could never receive stability, and a floor that restarts the chain does not help it. The link only passes a value along: a wall stack still needs an unbroken chain back to an anchor, and a wall stacked on an unsupported wall does not hold.

### Compromised pieces

A load-bearing piece that reaches the Critical state is Compromised, and fails on its own after a random delay with a warning (creak, dust, a small jiggle), only if it is a true bottleneck (GDD §7.6, D29). The test is a dry run of the cascade computation with the piece removed from the graph, run on a scratch copy of the stability map (`WouldBeBottleneck`): if at least one other piece would fall below the collapse threshold or lose its chain to an anchor, it is a bottleneck. A piece that merely lies on the best path of a neighbour that another path would still hold stays Critical but is not Compromised. The test runs once when the piece enters Critical and again after each batch of destructions (`RefreshCompromisedQueue`), so a piece whose dependants are gone has its countdown cancelled. The queue drains every tick like the collapse queue.

### Damage coupling (`c(h)`)

A piece's own damage now affects what it transmits upward, not just whether it collapses. `ComputeStability`/`RecomputeStability` multiply a neighbour's stability by `c(h)` before subtracting the link's loss - `c(h)` is `UDemolitionRules::ComputeStabilityCoupling(HPRatio)`, a list of points in `DA_DemolitionRules::StabilityTransmission`, `(0, 0.3)`, `(0.35, 0.6)` and `(0.75, 1.0)` by default, a straight line between two neighbours and flat beyond the ends (`PointCurve::Evaluate`, shared with the furniture value curve of the evaluator, so both data curves work the same way and are unit-tested without the engine). A list of fewer than two points is not a curve, and the default stands in. `UStructuralSupportComponent` reads it through `UDurabilityComponent::GetStabilityCoupling()` and never references `DA_DemolitionRules` directly - the graph doesn't know damage internals exist, only that a piece can report a coupling factor.

### Collapse queue and fracture

A piece no longer collapses the instant it's found unstable. `UnregisterPiece` records the grid cells it vacates (`RecentlyVacatedCells` - cells, not the piece itself, since it may already be mid-`Destroy()`). `RecomputeStability` runs a BFS (`ComputeHopDistances`) from those cells over the same socket-adjacency graph the relaxation walks, but over any link, not just load-bearing ones - hop distance is about physical distance, not who transmits stability to whom. Every newly-unstable piece found this pass gets a release time, `t = Now + BaseDelay + compressed(hops x HopDelay + jitter)`, where the compression scales down a big cascade's hop spread to fit `MaxCascadeSpread` without touching `BaseDelay`, a fixed reaction time shared by the whole batch. `TickComponent` drains this queue every tick, independent of the dirty-flag recompute, calling `BeginCollapse()` on whatever piece's release time has come up.

`BeginCollapse` is what actually detaches a piece: it unregisters from the graph (which can in turn destabilise whatever rested on it, picked up by the *next* recompute, so a cascade naturally continues wave by wave), starts simulating physics, and gives itself a horizontal impulse away from the centroid of its still-registered neighbours (`UStructuralSupportComponent::ComputeAwayFromNeighbourCentroid`, strength from `DA_DemolitionRules::CollapseImpulseStrength`) so a falling wall leans/slides outward instead of dropping straight down. It fractures on its first significant impact (`OnComponentHit` above `MinFractureImpactSpeed`) or, failing that, after a timeout (`CollapseLifeSpan`) - either way it is replaced by fragments, never just destroyed outright.

`ABuildablePiece::Fracture` is the one fracture entry point, used both by a direct tool-kill (fractures in place, no fall) and by a cascade release (fractures after falling). A mesh with an entry in `UDemolitionRules::MeshFractureOverrides` breaks into one of its authored Geometry Collections (cut in Chaos Fracture Mode, several variants per mesh with one picked at random, so a repeated piece does not always break the same way). Any other mesh falls back to a generic, procedural fragment set per `EPieceClass` (3-6 `AFragmentDebris`, GDD §7.8), each with a random offset/rotation and an outward impulse; for these shards the direction the piece fell away from (`CollapseBiasDirection`, zero for a direct kill) is blended into the outward impulse before scaling, so they scatter the way the piece fell, not independently of it. Either way the result registers with `UDebrisSubsystem`, which keeps the total fragment weight under `FragmentGlobalCap` (300) by destroying the oldest first; an authored collection weighs its chunk count, since it is one actor and not that many. A fragment times out and scales to zero over `FragmentFadeDuration` rather than fading its material's opacity, avoiding a translucent material entirely.

## House generation

`AHouseGenerator` builds a house in a plot from a `UHouseArchetype` (a Data Asset) and a seed. Everything goes through `APlot::PlacePiece`, the same function build mode uses, so a generated house is an ordinary house for the structural graph: it can be demolished, it collapses, it shows in the heat-map. A house type is data: a new one is a new asset, never new code.

**Determinism.** One `FRandomStream` seeded from the seed drives every choice, never the global random functions. The same seed and archetype give the same house; `GetLayoutHash()` fingerprints the result (class, cell and orientation of every piece, then class, position and yaw of every prop, in emission order), which is what the determinism test compares. A draw that is optional (the storey count, the roof shape, the standing) always consumes the same number of values, so what is drawn after it does not change when its weights do.

**Pipeline** (GDD §9.2):
1. **Footprint.** Sized within the archetype's bounds and what the plot can hold, centred on the plot with a seeded jitter. The front door goes on the side that faces `FacingTarget` (the truck's drop-off in a mission) when one is set.
2. **Storeys.** The foundation (also the ground floor), then storey by storey the floor slabs and the exterior walls: plain, or with a window variant at a seeded chance, and one door wall on the ground floor.
3. **Rooms.** Either one of the archetype's hand-written plans or, for an archetype with none, a random recursive split of the footprint with one door per cut, so every room is reachable. A plan (`UHouseLayout`) is a text grid per storey, one letter per room, with doors as links between rooms, flights of stairs, rooms that may swap type and an optional mirror. A plan that repeats its typical storeys (`bRepeatTypicalStorey`) is expanded to the storey count the seed draws in the archetype's range, which is how the apartment block gets its height from one plan.
4. **Support.** Every floor slab, and the ceiling slab laid under any roof, restarts the chain (see "Floors restart the chain"), so nothing is placed at an interval to keep a tall house up. Support posts go under any slab the graph says would fall, since partitions carry nothing and a slab more than one cell from an exterior wall has no support.
5. **Roof.** Flat, two-sided or four-sided, rolled from the archetype's weights and tilted by the footprint's proportions (a square leans towards four sides, a long house towards two). Pitched roofs come from a `URoofKit`, one Blueprint per role; a shape the footprint or the kit cannot make falls back to flat, with a log line.
6. **Validation.** `FindUnstablePieces` asks the graph whether anything of the finished house would fall. `GenerateValid` retries with seeds derived from the requested one (five attempts), then the archetype's known-good fallback seed, so a house always exists, and the same requested seed always ends on the same house. Only a validated house is furnished.
7. **Furnishing.** The rows of `DT_FurnishingRules` are grouped: one group is one object (a small, a normal and a large fridge), and a room gets at most one prop per group, picked among the rows of its room type by weight, then kept at a chance. A prop goes where its rule allows (against a wall, in a corner, centred, hung on a wall), inside the walls and clear of the doors and of what is already placed; what does not fit is skipped. The row owns every stat of the prop (material, hit points, fragments, mass, value), set on the actor as it is spawned, so the `BP_Prop_*` Blueprints only carry a mesh and its collision. Each house also rolls a standing (1 to 3) that tilts the quality tier of the furniture of every room, so a house is coherently modest or well off (GDD §9.4, D30).

Ceiling lamps are buildables with a `Ceiling` anchor, hung from the slab above each room. Props are plain physics actors, outside the structural graph.

## Missions

`AMissionDirector` is the one actor a mission level needs besides its plot and generator. When the level starts it reads the mission from the travel options (`Lvl_Mission?Seed=7?Archetype=twostorey?Type=partial?From=1?Floors=8`) and from its defaults, rebuilds the ground, builds the house with `GenerateValid`, binds the contract to it and switches the player's building side off. The truck in the hub only has to put the options in the URL. The seed drives both the ground and the house, so the same seed gives the same site; `pb.SetSeed` overrides it for demos and tests.

**Contract binding.** `MissionBinding::Compute` splits every item of the house, pieces and props, into the target set (to destroy) and the must-stay set (to leave alone). An item is a plain description (`FBindingItem`: piece class, load-bearing flag, storey, weight), not an actor, so the rules run and can be tested without a level. A *total* demolition targets everything. A *partial* one targets every item from a given storey up, except the floor slab of that storey, which becomes the ceiling of what stays; with fewer than two storeys it falls back to total and says so. A *renovation* targets the props and every piece that is not load-bearing, and the load-bearing structure stays. An item weighs the cost of its `DT_Shop` row. The sets are stored on the plot (`APlot::GetMissionSets`), which is where the mission report will read them.

**Contracts and offers.** A contract (`FContractDefinition`) is a request, not a house: client, type, archetype, house seed, footprint in cells, storeys, partial-from storey, conditions, base payout and tier. `UContractPool` (`DA_ContractPool`) holds weighted templates, each one a kind of job (small, standard and large bungalow, two and three storeys, apartment block) with a range for the footprint and the storeys, weights for the three types, and the chance and count of each condition. A new kind of job is a new row, not new code. `DrawContract` always makes the same draws in the same order, so one random stream gives the same contract again and a condition added later does not move what was already drawn. `UContractSubsystem` lives on the game instance, like the progress: it holds the three offers (drawn by weight, never the same kind twice in a set), the active contract and the last report, and renews the board when a mission ends. A kind above the player's tier is not offered (`pb.SetTier`; nothing earns tiers yet, reputation is Block 3). The area shown is nominal (cells x 4 m2 x storeys), because the generator may stretch a footprint to fit a plan; the real size is logged. The estimated time is 0.06 min per m2 times a type factor (partial 0.6, renovation 0.5), plus half a minute for each piece of furniture to carry out; the base payout is 12 per m2 times the same factor, and does not depend on the furniture (a template's `PayoutMultiplier` adjusts it). `AHouseGenerator::GenerateValid` takes an `FGenerationOverrides` (footprint, storeys) and only draws from the plans that can reach the size asked; the director reads the active contract first and falls back to the travel options and the editor values, so every older flow still works.

**Conditions.** *Salvage furniture*: the director picks K props with its own seeded stream, each draw weighted by the prop's value (the sofa is likelier than the lamp), and takes them out of the targets, and the report looks for them in the client drop zone. *Protected zone*: X of the Y `AProtectedZone` slots placed in the level are active (`AMissionDirector::ZoneCandidates`), drawn by seed before the ground is built so that a switched-off zone does not flatten its ground. *Keep standing*: a room type; the director picks one room of the house of that type and its pieces join the must-stay set. A room can only stay on the lowest storey that comes down (the ground floor in a total demolition, the first storey that goes in a partial one), since a room higher up would hang in the air once the storeys under it are gone; with no such room, nothing is kept and the summary says so. The pool asks for it only on a total demolition, because a partial demolition and a renovation already keep storeys or a shell, which the report counts together with the room. *Forbidden tool*: a tool id; the first damage dealt with it warns the player on screen and costs a share of what was earned, once for the whole mission.

**Evaluator and result.** `UContractEvaluator` is a set of pure functions: a `FMissionSnapshot` (plain numbers: the weight destroyed and in total for the targets and the must-stay set, the salvaged items, the zones, the counters) and the `FScoringParams` go in, an `FDebriefResult` with one line per term comes out. No actor and no level are involved, so the arithmetic is unit-tested in the editor (`Automation RunTests PlotBuilder.Contracts`). A zero denominator is defined, not left to chance: completion is 1 when there is nothing to destroy, collateral is 0 when nothing must stay.
- Completion = destroyed target weight / target weight. Earned = base payout x completion.
- Collateral = destroyed must-stay weight / must-stay weight. Zone damage = per active zone, the tool damage ratio plus the house weight that fell in over the whole house's weight times `ZoneFallFactor`, clamped to 1 and summed.
- Furniture loss = 1 - value kept / value asked. Only an item inside the client zone at the end counts, valued by a curve of its hit points (1.0 at full, 0.5 at 80 %, 0.05 at 0); everything else is lost.
- Safety = 0.1 per crush and 0.1 per explosion that caught the player, capped at 0.25. Forbidden tool = 0.15, once.
- Penalty = min(0.8, 1.5 x collateral + 1.5 x zone + 1.0 x furniture + safety + forbidden). Two terms also have a cap of their own, below the penalty cap, which still holds the total. The collateral term of a total demolition (the room kept standing) never goes above 40 % of what was earned (`CollateralCap`), because weighed 1.5 times it would otherwise take the whole 80 % after losing about half the room; a partial demolition and a renovation are held by the penalty cap alone. The furniture term never goes above 10 % of what was earned for each piece the client asked for (`FurnitureCapPerItem`), so three pieces to bring back cost at most 30 % while twenty reach the penalty cap. The line of the report says when one of these caps held.
- Upkeep = for each tool that worked, its flat fee plus its rate times its active seconds (see "Toolbelt and tools"). It comes off what is left once the penalty has been taken and never takes the payout below zero: the job is always paid.
- Payout = earned minus the penalty money minus the upkeep. Money is rounded one term at a time, so the lines of the report add up to the payout to the unit. The report has one line per term, plus an Upkeep line when a tool with a fee worked.

The coefficients are data (`UDemolitionRules::Scoring`). `UMissionResultSubsystem` lives on the world and records only what nobody else keeps (the safety events, the forbidden tool and the working time of the tools that have an upkeep); everything else is read once at the end, from the sets (an item whose actor is gone, or a piece that is already falling, is destroyed), the drop zone and the zones. `pb.EndMission` prints the report against the active contract, or against one made from the house that stands, and changes nothing: it can be run again at any time. `pb.Settle` does what the truck does (pays, closes the contract, keeps the report for the debrief) without travelling.

**Zones.** `ADropZone` is the client's drop-off: it keeps the live list of the props inside, nothing is locked, a prop thrown back out stops counting, and the report reads the list once, when the job ends. `AProtectedZone` watches every damageable thing inside it through its own `UDurabilityComponent` (hit points at the start, hit points lost, destroyed), so it counts whatever ends in `ApplyDamage`, a tool or a blast, without knowing the tools. What it holds is found by scanning its box by bounds (not by physics overlaps, which a kinematic shed does not always raise), and a thing whose hit points are set after the zone started is picked up on the next pass. The house falling into it is counted when a collapsing piece breaks: `ABuildablePiece` tells its plot (`APlot::NotifyPieceLanded`), the plot passes it on to its zones, and a zone whose box the piece touches counts the piece's weight once. The piece is what carries the weight and the identity, since its fragments vanish a few seconds later. `RecordExternalDamage` stays the generic door for any other source. `SetActive(false)` takes a zone out of the plot, stops its scan and hides what it holds. Both zones register themselves on the plot.

**Wall props.** The bathroom mirror and cabinet hang on a wall (`OnWall` in `DT_FurnishingRules`, height in the row). The generator keeps a spot only where a plain wall piece stands behind it, found by its grid coordinate (no door, no window), and `APhysicsProp::MountOn` stops the body from simulating. The wall tells the prop when it lets go: any damage to the wall, `ABuildablePiece::OnCollapseStarted`, or its destruction. The hands tool also takes a mounted prop off the wall before it grabs it.

**Ground.** `AMissionGround` builds the lot at the start of the level from the seed: flat under the plot (plus a margin), the zones, the player starts and every `AFlatZone` (the road, the yard), rolling beyond and fading in. The per-vertex weights of sand and dirt, and the distance to the nearest road zone, go into the vertex colours and the UVs, so one material tiles the textures and blends the road in without extra geometry. Scatter rows (trees, bushes, rocks, site clutter) are instanced meshes placed by seed, with a minimum spacing, optional clustering and a treeline along the edge; four invisible walls close the lot. Nothing is built in the editor: the level only shows the flat zones and the lot outline, and `pb.GenerateGround` rebuilds the ground at will.

**What the player reads.** Three pure layers sit between the report and the screens, so the numbers are tested without a level. `MissionNotebook::Build` turns the same snapshot into an `FNotebookData`: the contract (client, type, estimate, payout, the other conditions as short lines) and the live objectives (the completion, then one section per kind: the furniture with its state, in hands first, then to do, delivered and lost, the protected zones with their damage, the room to keep, the forbidden tool). `UMissionResultSubsystem::GetLiveNotebook` caches it and rebuilds it twice a second at most, so the ring chip and the notebook cost nothing when open all the time. `ContractTexts` writes the words (type, site, time to the half minute, the one-line job, each condition as a chip and a detail). `MissionDebrief::Build` turns an `FDebriefResult` into the waterfall of the debrief: the base payout, then one step per cause (the unfinished part, each penalty term, the cap), down to the payout. The steps add up to the payout to the unit, and when the 0.8 cap steps in the causes are shown for their share of what was really taken, never below zero.

**End of the mission and payment.** `UMissionResultSubsystem::Settle` runs once per mission, when the end truck leaves: it builds the report, passes it with the final notebook to `UContractSubsystem::CompleteActiveContract` (which closes the contract, renews the board and returns the money due), and `UProgressSubsystem::AddMoney` pays it. A mission with no active contract (a debug run) pays nothing. The report and the final notebook stay in `UContractSubsystem` until the hub has shown them (`HasUnseenResult`, `ConsumeUnseenResult`, `GetLastNotebook`, `GetLastPaid`).

## Hub, progress and the home

**Progress.** `UProgressSubsystem` lives on the game instance and holds the one live `UProgressSave`: money (500 for a new game), the ids of the owned tools, the parcel flag and the home snapshot. It writes the `Progress` slot at every change, which is rare (a payment, a purchase), so progress survives map changes and closing the game. `pb.ShowProgress` prints it, `pb.ResetProgress` starts a new game.

**Hub.** The things the player uses with E in the hub share one base, `AHubInteractable`: an invisible box on the interaction channel, a prompt shown while it is focused and a `Use` hook. `ATravelPoint` opens another level (the truck to a mission with a random seed, the home's access point, which refuses until the parcel is owned, and the way back), `ATerminal` is the PC: `Use` opens the terminal window on the HUD, or says that the PC does not start in a level whose HUD has no terminal screen. `AHubDirector` switches building off and locks the belt when the level starts.

**Travel.** `ATravelPoint::Use` broadcasts `UProgressSubsystem::OnBeforeLevelChange` and then opens the level, while the current world is still whole. The travel point does not know who listens, and the listener does not know who travels.

**Truck roles.** A travel point has a `TravelRole`, set on the placed actor (no subclass): `Plain` (a door), `StartMission` (the hub's truck) or `EndMission` (the mission's truck). The start truck refuses without an active contract ("Pick a contract at the PC first"; `bAllowWithoutContract` keeps the debug trip). The end truck settles the mission before travelling. A finished job leaves without a question. An unfinished one first asks: the HUD opens the "Leave the site?" card (`UGameHUDWidget::AskLeave`, Escape stays, E or the Leave button leaves and travels on), and a HUD with no such card falls back to a warning line and a second press of E within 5 seconds. A mission level with no `AMissionDirector` is treated as a plain door.

**The debrief at the hub.** On arrival `AHubDirector::ShowPendingDebrief` asks the HUD to show the report of the mission just ended, once. The HUD and the pawn are built after the level starts, so it retries every 0.2 s for 10 s; `pb.ShowDebrief` shows the last report again.

**Game flow.** `UGameFlowSubsystem` (on the game instance) says where the player is in the loop: `InHub`, `ContractSelected`, `OnSite`, `Debrief` or `InHome` (`EGameFlowState`). Nothing records the state as it happens. A few times a second (`PollSeconds`, 0.25) the subsystem reads four facts the game already has (the level holds a mission director, the level holds a home director, the debrief is the screen open on the HUD, a contract is taken and not completed) and `GameFlow::Resolve`, a pure function, works the state out of them, in priority order: a mission level is `OnSite` whatever else is true, then an open debrief is `Debrief`, then the home is `InHome`, then a taken contract is `ContractSelected`, anything else is `InHub`. A change is logged and announced on `OnStateChanged`. The state cannot drift from the game and no class has to remember to tell it anything; it also locks nothing, since what a map allows stays the three switches of the player. The HUD and the progress chip read the mission state from it. `pb.State` prints it with the facts behind it.

**The home.** `AHomeDirector` is the one actor `Lvl_Home` needs besides its plot. When the level starts it puts back what the player left: the pieces through `APlot::PlacePiece` (so every piece is registered in the graph), then the loose props, then the inventory through `UHotbarComponent::RestoreSlots` once the pawn exists. It takes a snapshot on `OnBeforeLevelChange`. It also sets the home's switches (demolition off, see "What a map allows").

What is saved:
- The pieces, from the graph's own list (`GetRegisteredPieces`, which excludes falling pieces and child actors such as door leaves and window panes): class, cell, quarter turns, roof step, paint index, and the scale they were placed with. That is `PlacedScale`, not the actor's current scale, which carries the corner and wall-clearance biases and would be biased a second time on every visit.
- The props that were not already in the level, in the plot's own space, so the plot can move in the level.
- The inventory grid, empty slots included, so it comes back as it was.

The records are dedicated `SaveGame` structs: a save only writes the properties that carry the flag, and those of `FInventorySlot` do not. `pb.SaveHome` takes a snapshot on demand, `pb.ClearHome` forgets it and `pb.SetParcel` stands in for the purchase of the parcel.

## Paint

The paint brush is the last slot of the tool belt and paints a placed piece with the colour of a paint bucket, an inventory item. Building turns it on and off (see "What a map allows").

- **Palette.** `UPaintPalette` is a Data Asset of named colours on the plot (`APlot::PaintPalette`). Entry 0 is the neutral one, which shows the material's own colour, so a piece that was never painted and a piece painted neutral are the same, the saved index defaults to 0, and painting with entry 0 takes a paint off.
- **On the piece.** `ABuildablePiece::ApplyPaint` writes the colour (Custom Primitive Data indices 2 to 4) and its blend (index 5). Indices 0 (stability) and 1 (damage) are not touched, so the damage tint stays on top of the paint. A piece stores an index, not a colour, so retuning the palette retunes every painted house, saved ones included.
- **Buckets.** `APaintBucket` is a marker class never spawned in the world; each colour is a Blueprint subclass with its palette index, one `DT_Shop` row in the `Paint` category (with `MaxOwned` 1, so the shop sells a colour once). The inventory, the shop and the home snapshot handle a bucket like any other item.
- **Brush.** `UPaintBrushComponent` paints the placed piece in focus with the bucket selected in the hotbar, names the colour on screen through `UPlayerMessages`, and says what is missing when there is no bucket or no palette. A bucket is not used up for now. Which colour to paint with is decided in one function (`ResolveColor`): if the player ever owns too many colours for the hotbar, a backpack of paints can answer there and nothing else changes.
- **Paint belongs to the placed piece.** A piece taken back and placed again is neutral. The home restore reapplies the index through the plot's palette.

## Chaos setup

Chaos gives the game its physical bodies and decides nothing: the structural graph chooses what falls and when, and Chaos only plays it out.

- **Rigid bodies.** Props, falling pieces, fragments and thrown charges are static meshes that simulate physics. A mesh needs simple collision (Auto Convex): one set to use its complex collision as simple silently refuses to simulate and drops impulses. A body spawned in a given frame may not be live in the solver yet, so the launch impulse of a generic fragment is applied 0.1 s later instead of at once.
- **Geometry Collections.** 11 meshes have authored collections, 19 in all: the wall, the interior wall, the floor and the roof slope have three cuts each, the foundation, the corner post, the flat roof, the stairs, both parapets and the window pane have one. Each is cut in Chaos Fracture Mode into 3 to 16 chunks; `MeshFractureOverrides` records the chunk count of each cut, which the debris cap uses as its weight. The cut is drawn at random when the piece breaks, but the cuts themselves are fixed, so a repeated piece costs nothing more per instance.
- **Breaking.** `ABuildablePiece::Fracture` spawns an `AGeometryCollectionActor` at the piece's own world rotation and scale (the window panes share one mesh and one collection, stretched per wall), pushes it (the way the piece fell, or an authored direction for a pane wedged in its frame), then calls `CrumbleActiveClusters` so every chunk is loose at once. Left alone, a light plate such as glass never reaches the collection's damage threshold and would stay in one piece.
- **End of life.** The collection actor registers with `UDebrisSubsystem` and disappears after `FragmentLifetime`. It has no fade, unlike the generic shards, because a Chaos cluster has no clean uniform-scale hook: it pops out.

## User interface

The interface is UMG with its logic in C++. Its C++ base, `UGameHUDWidget`, opens one menu at a time (`OpenMenu`, `CloseMenu`, `ToggleMenu`), shows the cursor and ignores move and look input while a menu is open; the Blueprint only lays out the money chip, the tool belt column, the hotbar, the controls hint and the screens. ### Look

`UUIStyle` (`DA_UIStyle`) holds the whole look in one Data Asset: the colours (written in sRGB, as a colour picker shows them, and converted), the colour and icon of each shop category, one icon per tool, and the padlock, search and close icons. `FItemLook` turns a catalog row or an inventory stack into what a widget draws (name, category, colour, icon); a paint bucket takes its colour from the plot's palette through `UPaintBrushComponent::GetBucketColor`. The panels are slate with one orange accent. Text is Barlow, with Barlow Condensed for headings, numbers and keys, as two composite fonts (`F_Barlow`, `F_BarlowCondensed`). The 14 icons (`T_Icon_*` in `Content/UI/Icons`) are pictograms drawn for the project and stand in for real icons; `UUIStyle::MakeRounded` builds the rounded frames, so no frame is an asset.

### Shop

`UShopWidget` builds one `UShopRowWidget` per `DT_Shop` row once. Filtering by category or by the search text then only switches each row between Visible and Collapsed; rebuilding the list on every keystroke made the menu lag, and the rows are reordered only when the sort changes. The category buttons show how many items they hold, the cross at the end of the search field empties it, and Escape first empties the field and only then closes the menu.

The right panel is the cart (see "Cart" above): the lines with a remove button, the total, the balance (an infinity sign in infinite-money mode), what is left after the purchase, the room in the bag, and Confirm. Confirm is disabled while the player cannot pay or the items do not fit, and the figure that blocks it (what is left, or the room in the bag) turns to the danger colour; a purchase is confirmed by a line that goes away after a few seconds. A row's plus button is disabled once the purchase limit is reached, so a paint colour already held cannot be added.

### Inventory, hotbar and tool belt

One `UItemSlotWidget` class serves both grids. In the HUD it is only drawn (hit-test invisible). In the drawer a left click calls `OnSlotLeftClicked` and a right click `OnSlotRightClicked` on `UHotbarComponent`, which owns what a click does, and the slot shows a tooltip built on each hover so it describes the stack as it is now. `UCursorStackWidget` is the same slot widget pointed at the cursor's stack (`GetCursorSlot`) instead of a grid index: it redraws on `OnCursorSlotChanged`, shows itself while the cursor holds something, and `WBP_HUD` moves it under the pointer. `UInventoryWidget` and `UHotbarWidget` size their grids from `GetTotalSlotCount()`, which is known from the settings before the inventory has made its grid. When the selected hotbar slot changes, the name of the item shows in a label for 5 seconds and then goes away by itself, so it takes no room while the player works; the whole hotbar hides on a map that does not allow building.

`UToolbeltBarWidget` draws one tile per owned tool and a padlock tile for the missing ones (`CountMissingTools`; its tooltip says to buy them at the terminal in the hub). A tool the player owns but the map does not allow is dimmed (`IsSlotAvailable`). When the equipped tool changes, its tile shows a chip with the tool's name and what the two clicks do, for 5 seconds. The same widget class serves two layouts: in a column (the HUD) the tiles keep their size, in a row (the drawer) they take a Fill slot each and share the width.

### What the game says to the player

`UPlayerMessages` (a world subsystem) holds the words the player must read: the prompt of the thing in front of them ("[E] Go", the brush's colour) and up to four short messages that explain a refusal ("You do not own a parcel yet"). Gameplay code only says what and when; `APlotBuilderHUD` draws it as plain canvas text, scaled to the screen height. It needs no asset and works in every build, and a widget can replace it by reading the same two getters without any caller changing. If the level's HUD class is not `APlotBuilderHUD`, the service says so once in the log and falls back to the engine's on-screen text.

The text is drawn just above the hotbar, with the short messages stacked upward from the prompt. It does not guess a screen position: `UHotbarWidget` reports where its own geometry begins to `UPlayerMessages::SetBottomLimit` every frame, and the HUD places the prompt from that, so the text follows the layout when the hotbar changes. With no hotbar on screen (a mission) it falls back to a fixed share of the screen height.

This is deliberately not debug output. Text meant for the developer goes through `PBDebug::Message`, which `pb.Debug` can switch off; text the player must read goes through `UPlayerMessages` and is never touched by that switch. The two used to share `GEngine->AddOnScreenDebugMessage`, which is why neither could be turned off without the other.

### The focus card

The card tells the player what they are looking at: the name, the material and the storey of a piece of the house (or the role of a piece of furniture), a health bar, and the lines that help a decision: the multiplier of the tool in hand against that material (from the tool x material matrix), a warning when the structure has given up on the piece and it is about to fall (`ABuildablePiece::IsCompromised`), the value of a piece of furniture the client asked for with the share of it the health curve leaves, and whether the take-back key does something on that target. The text is a pure function: `FocusCard::Describe` takes `FFocusCardFacts` (plain values) and returns the lines with a tone each (fine, worth a look, bad), so the wording and the places where the card shows are covered by automation tests with no level. `UFocusCardWidget` only gathers the facts from the interaction focus, the toolbelt and the game flow, and draws the result. The card shows in a mission and in the home, not in the hub, and is hidden while a menu is open.

### Opening and closing menus

Each toggle has its own input action: `IA_ToggleShop`, `IA_ToggleInventory` and `IA_ToggleHelp`, read by the player controller Blueprint on their Triggered pin, which calls `ToggleMenu` or `ToggleHelp` on the HUD. An action with no trigger is Triggered on every frame its key is down, so a tap of a few frames toggled the menu several times and what the player saw depended on whether the count was odd or even. Each of the three has a Pressed trigger, so Triggered fires once per press, however long the key is held.

Inside a menu, Escape and the menu's own key close it, handled in `NativeOnKeyDown`. `B` does not close the shop while a letter is typed in the search field. The auto-repeat of a held key is ignored there: the key that opened the menu may still be down, and its repeats used to close the menu, which made the controller see a new press and reopen it, so the screen flickered for as long as the key was held.

The HUD is the host of every screen. `UGameHUDWidget` (the C++ base of `WBP_HUD`) opens one screen at a time by name (`OpenMenu`, `CloseMenu`, `ToggleMenu`) and is the only place that changes the input mode: opening a screen puts the game in UI-only input with the cursor shown and move and look ignored, closing it undoes that and hands the cursor stack back to the bag, so two screens can never fight over the mode. Screens that are not the shop or the bag are opened by the thing they belong to: the PC calls `OpenMenu("Terminal")`, the hub director calls `ShowDebrief`, the end truck calls `AskLeave`. Their common base `UGameScreenWidget` closes on Escape or its own key and ignores key repeat. The notebook key is the one input bound in C++: `APlotBuilderPlayerController` binds `IA_ToggleNotebook` (N, in `IMC_Mission`) on `Started` and calls `ToggleMenu("Notebook")`, which refuses outside a mission. The screens whose widget a HUD does not have are optional (`BindWidgetOptional`), so a HUD without them still works.

### Controls hint

`UControlsHintWidget` (`WBP_ControlsHelp`) is a reminder of the keys: a card of key caps grouped as Move, Tools, Menus and Build mode, and a small "H Controls" chip while the card is folded. It starts folded, and `H` opens and folds it. The groups follow what the map allows, read when the card opens: Tools is hidden where the belt is locked (the hub) and Build mode where building is off (a mission).

`H` still goes through `UGameHUDWidget::ToggleHelp`, which shows and hides the widget with `SetVisibility`. The widget overrides it: Collapsed or Hidden folds the card, any other value opens it, and the widget itself always stays on screen because its folded state is the chip. That kept the Blueprint as it was. The hint is a stand-in for a real onboarding and is meant to be thrown away, so it stays this simple.

## Performance

Measured numbers and the method live in [`PROFILING.md`](PROFILING.md), one dated entry per session. Headline of the first one (T5, a three-storey house of 233 pieces, 2026-11-06): the graph recompute is coalesced (at most one per frame, 17.64 ms at its worst), Chaos stays under 5 ms, the fragment cap of 300 holds, and the visible cost of destroying everything in one frame is on the render side (`UpdateAllPrimitiveSceneInfos`). The cause of the worst frame of a real cascade (64 ms) is still open.

Named CPU scopes `Structural.Tick` and `Structural.Recompute` are in `UStructuralSupportComponent`; `pb.CollapseAll [fall|break]` fires the worst case on demand and leaves a trace bookmark.

## Key technical choices

- **Component separation over one big Blueprint**: `UInteractionComponent` (generic detection) and `UPhysicsManipulationComponent` (physics manipulation) are deliberately two components, not one - detection and manipulation are different responsibilities, and neither needs to know how the other works.
- **C++ for the skeleton, Blueprint for variants**: base classes, interfaces, and components are C++; each buildable piece or prop is a Blueprint subclass assigning a mesh and tuning values, never new code.
- **Two snapping strategies, chosen per piece**: the flush snap, computed from each mesh's own bounding box (no per-piece authoring), stays for pieces that do not need a grid; the grid path is used for the construction kit, where pieces must meet on exact sockets. A piece opts in through its build anchor, so nothing built around the flush snap had to change.
- **Sockets are data in the Blueprint, not sockets on the mesh**: a mesh socket follows the mesh, so replacing a greybox mesh by a final one would break the graph. Keeping them in the piece's data, relative to its pivot, makes a mesh swap a reference change.
- **Integer coordinates everywhere in the portance logic**: a single conversion from a piece to integer socket coordinates, shared by Build Mode, the debug view and the graph, instead of comparing floating-point positions.
- **Portance as a propagation graph, not a physics simulation**: stability is cheap, predictable, readable on screen (the ghost's colour) and easy to explain, at the cost of not simulating real loads.
- **A second vertical axis for roofs rather than a finer floor grid**: roofs rise in 100 cm steps while storeys are 300 cm; `RoofStep` keeps `FloorHeight` meaning one thing for every other piece.
- **Greybox first, assets later**: every piece is a simple, thick, closed mesh with its pivot and thickness fixed by the design, so the logic does not depend on final art and replacing meshes is a data change.
- **The player doesn't block its own construction**: the obstruction check deliberately ignores the player's own capsule. An earlier version blocked on it too, which made it near-impossible to build next to yourself - no FPS building game self-blocks for this reason either.
- **UI driven by events, not tick**: the component broadcasts changes and widgets re-read state, so the UI cannot drift from the inventory. The only per-frame work is the bind retry until the pawn exists and the hotbar reporting its top edge.
- **Widget logic in C++, layout in Blueprint**: each widget Blueprint is a layout of named widgets under a C++ base class, and the whole look is one Data Asset (`UUIStyle`). A restyle never touches code, and the logic is readable and testable in one place instead of spread over graphs.
- **A cart in front of the purchase**: the shop collects lines and the inventory quotes and buys them all or none, so the shop widget needs no rule of its own about money or room.
- **A toggle key needs a Pressed trigger**: an action without a trigger is Triggered on every frame its key is down, which toggled a menu several times per tap. The fix sits in the input assets, so no Blueprint graph changed.
- **Tools never see a concrete class**: every demolition tool resolves a hit to `IDamageable` and stops there - a tool added later (or a breakable prop) needs no change to `ADemolitionTool`/`AExplosiveCharge`, only an `ApplyDamage_Implementation`.
- **The structural graph doesn't know damage exists, and damage doesn't know the graph exists**: `c(h)` lives on `DA_DemolitionRules`, read through a one-float forwarder (`UDurabilityComponent::GetStabilityCoupling`) - moving it onto `DA_StructuralRules` instead would have made the graph need to know about HP ratios, breaking that split for a value that is really "how damaged is this material", not a portance concept.
- **A generic fragment fallback under authored per-mesh fracture**: GDD §7.8 asks for per-mesh debris, which costs a Geometry Collection to author per mesh. Only the common meshes get one (`MeshFractureOverrides`); every other piece, and every prop, takes one procedural shard shape per `EPieceClass`, so anything can break without waiting for its asset. `UDebrisSubsystem` caps both kinds together, and does not care which version produced a fragment.
- **Collapse is a queue, not an instant cascade**: a piece becomes unstable on one recompute and only detaches at its own staggered release time, so a multi-piece failure reads as waves instead of everything vanishing on the same frame - the direction bias and the fracture delay both lean on that same release moment existing as a distinct event.
- **One placement function for everything that creates pieces**: build mode, the house generator and the home restore all call `APlot::PlacePiece`. A piece spawned any other way is invisible to the graph (nothing holds it up, nothing can collapse it), so a generated house or a restored home needed no graph code of its own.
- **Generation is deterministic and validated, not hoped for**: one seeded stream, a layout hash to prove it, and a stability check of the finished house with bounded re-rolls and a known-good fallback seed. A house that cannot stand is never shown to the player.
- **House types, plans, roofs and furniture are data**: an archetype, a plan, a roof kit and a furnishing row each are an asset or a table row. The generator never names a class; the `BP_Prop_*` Blueprints carry no stat, the row does.
- **Systems that travel together do not know each other**: the travel point broadcasts that the level is about to change and the home director listens; a tool damages through `IDamageable` and the graph reads damage through a forwarder. Neither side of those pairs names the other.
- **What a map allows is three independent switches**: the belt lock, building (painting included) and destruction. The earlier single lock also meant "this is a build-only level" to the input code; splitting it lets a map mix them, such as a renovation that builds and demolishes.
- **Player text and developer text are separate channels**: `UPlayerMessages` for what the player must read, `PBDebug::Message` behind `pb.Debug` for everything else, so a demo build can hide all debug output and still show the prompts.
- **A paint is an index into the plot's palette, not a colour**: the save stays small and a palette change reaches every house, saved ones included.
- **The score is pure arithmetic on plain numbers**: the evaluator takes a snapshot of weights and counters and returns a report, with no actor and no level. It can be tested without playing, each line of the report can be checked by hand, and the coefficients can be tuned in a data asset.
- **A contract is a request, the generator answers it**: the contract carries a footprint, storeys and a seed, and the generator builds the closest house its plans allow. The offer's area is therefore nominal and the real size is logged, instead of the board promising a number the generator cannot always keep.
- **Count what carries identity, not what is left**: the debris of a collapsing piece vanishes within seconds and raises no overlap event, so a protected zone counts the piece itself at the moment it breaks. The weight and the "once" come from the piece, and no fragment is tracked.
- **Wall props are held by their wall, not glued to it**: a hung prop only stops simulating, and the wall tells it when to let go. No attachment, no joint, no polling.
- **The state of the loop is derived, not stored**: where the player is (hub, contract taken, on site, debrief, home) is worked out a few times a second from facts that already exist, by a pure function. A stored state would have to be kept in step by every class that changes the facts, and one of them would forget.
- **A floor restarts the chain, instead of a special piece doing it**: the flag sits on the floor slab itself, not on a second slab Blueprint placed every few storeys, so a tall house holds with nothing to place or tune and the player's own slabs follow the same rule. The slab still falls like any floor when its own support gives out.
- **Caps per term, then a cap on the total**: the furniture and the kept room each have a ceiling of their own, because a few pieces to bring back, or one room to keep, could otherwise take most of the pay on their own. The penalty cap of 80 % still holds the sum, and the report says which cap held.

## Known limitations

- Destroying a whole house in one frame (a stress case, not gameplay) hitches for 190 to 340 ms, in the renderer's primitive update; a real cascade peaks at 64 ms. Fragments are spawned first and evicted past the cap afterwards, so a large burst creates and destroys primitives in the same frame. Details and candidate fixes in [`PROFILING.md`](PROFILING.md); the optimisation pass is Block 2.
- An authored collection disappears with a hard pop at the end of its life, where the generic shards scale to zero.
- Roofs: rectilinear footprints only (the generator builds flat, two-sided and four-sided roofs; the concave corner piece, `BP_Roof_Valley`, is never used), no overhang, no rafters (roof span is limited only by the portance rules), and the decorative ridge (`BP_Roof_Ridge`, not a grid piece) is only placed by hand.
- A depth offset in the material is the last resort against coplanar faces, to be evaluated in Block 2 (GPU cost, interaction with virtual geometry): the overlap fixes (see "Grid path") are all geometric biases.
- `APlot` is still a single bounds volume: the parcel is a flag in the saved progress, not a plot with an owner, a cost or a sale (the long-term vision for that is out of scope for now).
- The interface has no animations, and its icons are pictograms drawn for the project, to be replaced by real ones (the Furniture Kit ships previews for the props). The widget Blueprints are laid out by hand or from an editor script, and they have proved fragile (an editor crash when a loaded one is deleted and recreated, a name collision that corrupts a tree); a layout built in C++ is the candidate for after the Block 1 hand-in. The controls hint is a stand-in for an onboarding, and its key labels are written in the Blueprint, so they do not follow a remapped key (there is no remapping yet).
- Painting belongs to the placed piece only: a piece taken back and placed again is neutral. Door leaves and window panes (child actors) are not painted, the fragments of a painted piece do not take its colour, the brush has no held mesh yet, and a bucket is not used up.
- Tool ownership is enforced only where a director turns it on (hub, mission, home); `Lvl_DemoTest` keeps every tool. A tool cannot be sold back, and the upkeep is a first model (a flat fee and a rate per second of work, taken from the payout after the penalty) that has not been balanced over several runs. Charges are the only consumable: a second one would need its own stock.
- The contract loop plays end to end (board, truck, mission, notebook, leave card, payment, debrief), but its balance is a first pass: the coefficients, the estimated times and the payouts have not been tuned over several runs. The portrait of a client is an initials disc on its tint colour; real portraits are Block 3.
- A protected zone counts the pieces that fall into it but not their fragments, which vanish within seconds and raise no overlap event. Pieces break within one or two metres of the house, so a zone has to sit close to it to be reached. A zone is also checked by its box, so a piece that only touches it counts in full (`bCountTouching`).
- The area of an offer is nominal (cells x 4 m2 x storeys). A plan stretches a footprint only so far, and the generator only draws from the plans that can reach the size asked, but the built footprint can still differ a little; the real size is in the log. Nothing makes the offer match it.
- *Keep standing* is one room of a named type, and the pool offers it on a total demolition only. A façade or a single wall cannot be asked to stay.
- The tier of the board has no source yet: it stays 0 unless `pb.SetTier` moves it, and it is not saved.
- The home is saved when the player leaves through a travel point (or with `pb.SaveHome`), not on Escape or when Play In Editor is stopped. A piece placed by hand in the editor is not in the graph, so it is not saved, and `Lvl_Home` must hold no pickable prop placed in the editor (it would duplicate with the inventory). A Legacy-anchored piece cannot go through the shared placement function and is skipped, and counted in the summary.
- A piece placed by hand in the editor is not in the structural graph: a grid piece only gets its coordinate through `APlot::PlacePiece`, and a registration pass at `BeginPlay` would silently put every hand-placed piece at (0, 0, 0). Houses are generated (`pb.SpawnHouse`) or restored, not placed by hand.
- Impact damage on props is set per prop Blueprint, not per material, and its default values are first estimates that still have to be tuned against real throws and collapses. Only the bathroom mirror and cabinet are wall-mounted; a prop on a wall hangs from that wall alone (a prop spanning two wall pieces follows the one behind its middle).
- The legacy Grab/Push bindings are gated on the belt lock, and are to be removed: the Hands tool already does the grab, and nothing needs the push. `GrabAction`/`PushAction` live in `IMC_Build`, alongside the toolbelt's own `PrimaryUse`/`SecondaryUse` in `IMC_Mission`; both contexts stay active everywhere, and `UToolbeltComponent::IsLocked()` decides which one acts. Only the hub locks the belt, so the legacy bindings are live there; everywhere else, the home included, the toolbelt wins, and the push of an unheld prop does not exist. A real per-level Enhanced Input setup is the eventual fix.
